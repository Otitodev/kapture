# Product Demo Style Systems

These are visual directions, not licensed asset libraries. Study the linked work for composition and motion; create original graphics and use the product's own brand. Check that a reference still shows the intended work before using it. Choose one system per cut unless a deliberate change marks a chapter.

## 1. Playful editorial product motion

Use for energetic browser tools, creator products, and quick feature tours.

- **Composition:** Oversized left-aligned headline, highlighted phrase, tilted product card on the right, small scene metadata, persistent brand marker.
- **Look:** Warm or pastel full-frame colors, dark ink, rounded rectangles, thin outlines, offset shadows, bold geometric sans type.
- **Motion:** Headline lines rise in sequence; product card enters from the side and gently floats. Cut on musical accents; keep each feature on screen long enough to read.
- **Proof:** Product UI should occupy a substantial part of the card. Replace a static image with a tight real interaction when motion clarifies the feature.
- **Reference:** [Dropbox motion guidelines](https://brand.dropbox.com/motion) — study purposeful movement and subtle playfulness.

## 2. Minimal premium

Use for products where precision, calm, and detail matter.

- **Composition:** One strong screen or device frame, generous whitespace, short headline, sparse supporting copy.
- **Look:** Neutral palette, restrained accent color, fine borders, crisp typography, subtle depth.
- **Motion:** Slow camera push or panel reveal, precise easing, fewer cuts. Let one interaction complete before changing scenes.
- **Proof:** Favor clean full-resolution product footage and close crops over decorative graphics.
- **Reference:** [Apple MacBook Pro](https://www.apple.com/macbook-pro/) — study the product-first framing and restrained pacing of its film and highlights.

## 3. Dark technical

Use for developer tools, data products, and workflows with meaningful state changes.

- **Composition:** Dark canvas, layered interface crops, concise labels or step numbers, occasional full-screen UI reveal.
- **Look:** High-contrast text, one luminous accent, grids or rules only when they organize information.
- **Motion:** Masked reveals, cursor or selection focus, synchronized state changes. Avoid constant ambient movement around dense UI.
- **Proof:** Show the input, action, and resulting state; zoom only far enough to keep the relevant text legible.
- **Reference:** [Linear's interface design refresh](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh) — study how supporting UI recedes so the task stays in focus.

## 4. Kinetic typography

Good for a short, message-led launch with few available product screens.

- **Composition:** Words and phrases dominate the frame; UI appears as brief evidence between statements.
- **Look:** Strong type scale, high-contrast brand colors, limited graphic motifs.
- **Motion:** Build sentences by rhythm and meaning; vary scale, mask, and position rather than applying one preset to every word.
- **Proof:** Place a product action immediately after each strong claim.
- **Reference:** [BUCK's Slack work](https://buck.co/work/slack) — study punchy messaging joined to playful animation.

## 5. Tactile collage

Good for collaborative, creative, or human-centered tools.

- **Composition:** Layer UI with cutout imagery, paper shapes, hands, and editorial type in a shallow stage.
- **Look:** Texture, imperfect edges, warm color, deliberate overlap.
- **Motion:** Slide, peel, fold, or reposition layers as if handled physically; use stop-motion-like holds.
- **Proof:** Keep the real UI readable within the collage and let it drive the story.
- **Reference:** [BUCK's Asana Brand Film](https://buck.co/work/asana-brand-film) — study its mid-century collage and hands manipulating UI elements.

## 6. Spatial 3D interface

Good for a hero launch when the product benefits from a sense of space or transformation.

- **Composition:** UI planes or devices occupy a simple 3D stage with a clear foreground and background.
- **Look:** Controlled lighting, material depth, sparse typography, brand-colored environments.
- **Motion:** Move a camera or object through one understandable transformation; return to a flat screen for detailed proof.
- **Proof:** Preserve true product screens on surfaces; avoid 3D spectacle that obscures the workflow.
- **Reference:** [BUCK's Microsoft app films](https://buck.co/work/microsoft-app) — study spatial transitions built around actual app benefits.

## 7. Character-led illustration

Good when the product already has a mascot or a strong illustrated identity.

- **Composition:** A character introduces a problem, reacts to a product action, and gives the scene a memorable payoff.
- **Look:** Consistent shapes, expressions, and backgrounds drawn from the product's own visual language.
- **Motion:** Use specific poses and reactions; leave room for the UI to remain the evidence.
- **Proof:** Connect the character's reaction to an authentic product result.
- **Reference:** [Duolingo's character design story](https://blog.duolingo.com/building-character/) — study how character animation supports in-app feedback.

## 8. Documentary walkthrough

Good for tools whose real interaction is more persuasive than a graphic metaphor.

- **Composition:** Mostly full-screen recording with selective zooms, brief labels, and optional presenter inset.
- **Look:** Clean screen capture, visible cursor, restrained annotations, consistent caption treatment.
- **Motion:** Cut idle time; follow the cursor or action; pause on the result before moving on.
- **Proof:** Show the task from starting state to completed result without inventing steps.
- **Reference:** [Loom's product demo example](https://www.loom.com/community/product-demo) — study the context-rich walkthrough structure.

## 9. Diagrammatic systems

Good for automation, integrations, and multi-step workflows.

- **Composition:** Nodes, paths, labels, and UI panels arranged on a disciplined grid.
- **Look:** Limited palette, consistent line weights, modular spacing, clear information hierarchy.
- **Motion:** Reveal a flow in causal order; morph a diagram into the matching UI state.
- **Proof:** Tie each connection or data movement to a real feature and result.
- **Reference:** [BUCK's IBM Design Language films](https://buck.co/work/ibm-design-language) — study how grids and motion explain a system.

## 10. Live-action hybrid

Good when a real-world use case or human outcome should lead the story.

- **Composition:** Open on a person, place, or task; introduce UI as an overlay, device screen, or match cut.
- **Look:** Coherent footage color and lighting; graphics inherit the product's brand without covering the action.
- **Motion:** Cut between physical action and the corresponding product action; keep transitions motivated by the story.
- **Proof:** Show the real product resolving the situation established in the footage.
- **Reference:** [BUCK's Asana Silos](https://buck.co/work/asana-silos) — study its transition from live-action setting to product UI.

## Choosing and extending

Describe a new style with the same four decisions: composition, look, motion, and proof. Change at least composition and motion when creating a genuinely different template. Keep scene facts independent from style so the same product story can be rendered in more than one design. External references are inspiration only: do not reuse their footage, music, logos, or character designs without rights.
